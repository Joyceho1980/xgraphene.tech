## Definition

ATP, or adenosine triphosphate, is a small molecule that cells use as an immediate energy carrier. It stores usable chemical energy in phosphate bonds and releases that energy when converted to ADP and inorganic phosphate.

ATP is constantly regenerated. Cells do not store large amounts of ATP for long-term use; instead, they continually recycle it through metabolic pathways.

## Quick Answer

ATP is the main short-term energy carrier used by cells. When ATP is broken down to ADP and phosphate, it releases energy that can power biological work such as membrane transport, muscle relaxation and contraction, protein synthesis, and cell signaling. Cells constantly regenerate ATP from nutrients through pathways including glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.

## Why It Matters

ATP is the practical link between metabolism and function.

Food energy is not directly usable by most cellular machines. Cells first convert nutrient-derived energy into ATP or related energy carriers. ATP then powers specific tasks inside the cell.

This makes ATP central to:

1. Nerve signaling.
2. Muscle activity.
3. Protein synthesis.
4. Repair processes.
5. Ion pumps.
6. Immune activation.

## Mechanism

ATP is used when a cell couples ATP hydrolysis to work.

Common ATP-dependent processes include:

1. Na+/K+-ATPase maintaining ion gradients.
2. Myosin cycling during muscle contraction and relaxation.
3. Molecular transport across membranes.
4. Protein phosphorylation in signaling pathways.
5. Biosynthesis of proteins, DNA, RNA, and lipids.

ATP is regenerated through multiple pathways:

1. Glycolysis in the cytosol.
2. TCA cycle metabolism in mitochondria.
3. Oxidative phosphorylation at the inner mitochondrial membrane.
