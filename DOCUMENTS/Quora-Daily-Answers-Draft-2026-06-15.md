## Question 1

**Why do I still feel tired after sleeping?**

Feeling tired after sleeping does not always mean you slept too few hours.

The first thing to separate is sleep duration from sleep quality. A person can spend enough time in bed and still wake up tired if the body's recovery processes were interrupted or incomplete. This can happen with fragmented sleep, stress activation, breathing irregularities, or circadian disruption.

Biologically, sleep is an active recovery state. During sleep, the body shifts away from external activity and toward internal regulation. Energy balance is being restored, immune activity is being regulated, and cellular repair processes are ongoing. Mitochondria are part of this process because they support ATP production, which cells need for recovery.

If sleep is repeatedly interrupted, some of those recovery cycles may start but not fully complete. That can leave a person feeling tired even when the total sleep time looks adequate on paper.

Sleep quality is not a single variable. It is the outcome of multiple biological systems synchronizing over time.

## Question 2

**Why does inflammation slow healing?**

Inflammation slows healing when it remains active longer than the body needs it to.

At the beginning of healing, inflammation is useful. It helps protect damaged tissue, remove debris, and signal that repair needs to begin. Without that early response, normal healing would be weaker, not better.

The problem begins when the body has difficulty moving from the early defensive phase into the later repair phase. In biological terms, healing depends not only on mounting an inflammatory response, but also on resolving it at the right time.

This transition involves immune signaling, changes in cellular activity, and shifts in how the local tissue environment is regulated. When that resolution process is delayed, tissue repair can also be delayed.

Inflammation is not the problem. Failed resolution is.
