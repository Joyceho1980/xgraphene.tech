## Answer 1

Question: I eat well and sleep for 7-8 hours. Why do I still feel tired?

Eating well and sleeping 7-8 hours are important, but they do not guarantee that the body has fully recovered.

The more useful distinction is between inputs and recovery.

A person can eat reasonably well and spend enough time in bed, yet still feel tired if sleep quality, stress load, oxygen delivery, or cellular energy production is not working efficiently.

Biologically, sleep is not a passive state. During deeper sleep, the body shifts toward repair and regulation. Energy balance is restored, immune activity is adjusted, and the brain clears metabolic waste more actively.

If sleep is repeatedly interrupted, those processes may begin without fully finishing.

Food works the same way in a different layer. Eating well provides raw materials, but cells still have to convert those materials into usable energy. That process depends on mitochondrial function, oxygen delivery, hormonal regulation, and inflammation level.

That is why someone can appear to be doing the obvious things right and still feel tired. The body may not be short on food or sleep time. It may be struggling with recovery efficiency.

This does not explain every case. Persistent fatigue can also involve anemia, thyroid issues, sleep apnea, depression, chronic stress, medication effects, or other medical conditions.

But as a general biological explanation, the key point is simple:

Fatigue is not always a sign that you are missing inputs; sometimes it is a sign that the recovery system is not converting those inputs efficiently.

## Answer 2

Question: Why does inflammation slow healing?

Inflammation slows healing when it stays active longer than the body needs it to.

At the beginning of healing, inflammation is useful. It helps protect damaged tissue, clear debris, and signal that repair needs to begin. Without that early response, normal healing would be weaker, not stronger.

The problem starts when the body has difficulty moving out of that early defensive phase.

Healing is not just the activation of inflammation. It also depends on resolution. Immune cells need to shift from clearing and defending toward rebuilding and repair. The local tissue environment also has to become calm enough for new tissue formation.

When inflammatory signaling remains elevated, repair becomes less efficient. Blood flow, oxygen delivery, collagen formation, and cellular energy use can all be affected. The tissue may still be trying to heal, but it is doing so in an environment that is not fully ready for rebuilding.

This is one reason chronic inflammation is different from acute inflammation. Acute inflammation is part of repair. Chronic or prolonged inflammation can interfere with repair timing.

So the issue is not that inflammation exists.

The issue is whether the body can resolve it at the right time.

Inflammation is not the problem; failed resolution is.
